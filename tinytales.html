What is Tiny Tales?

Tiny Tales is an iPad app concept designed during a design sprint to solve a very real bedtime problem: parents and children often find it hard to quickly choose a story to read.

We spend more time deciding what to read than actually reading.

The problem

Decision fatigue before the storytelling

Parents and children want different things from the same ten minutes, and the gap between them is where bedtime stalls.

Kids want

  • Fun
  • Stories that match their mood
  • Immediate options

Parents want

  • Meaningful stories — values, learning, representation
  • Age-appropriate content
  • Quick decisions at bedtime
  • Less mental load

Research

Bedtime is when parents are most tired

I interviewed parents about how story time actually goes, rather than how they would like it to go. The same few things came up every time.

Key insights

  • Bedtime is when parents are most tired.
  • Too many books = decision paralysis.
  • Parents want stories with purpose (women empowerment, history, kindness, diversity).
  • Kids choose based on mood, not logic.
  • Parents often end up re-reading the same story to avoid the hassle.

The most telling finding was the workaround: rather than face the choice, parents often end up re-reading the same story night after night to avoid the hassle. Meanwhile children were not choosing logically at all — they were choosing by mood.

Competitive analysis

Nobody had simplified the decision

I looked at what parents were already using. Each app had a different problem, but none of them had the same problem I was trying to solve.

ApplicationProblem observed
Epic!Huge library, overwhelming browsing
Amazon KindleNot child-decision friendly
Bedtime StoriesFeels like a game

No app simplified the decision process itself.

Core insight

  • The problem was not lack of stories.
  • The problem was too many stories and no quick way to choose.
  • This led to the concept of a Three-Tap Solution.

I was not building a better library. I was building a faster decision.

Ideation

Eight ideas in eight minutes

After synthesising the interviews and the competitor analysis, I moved into rapid ideation using the Crazy 8s method — eight concepts, fast, before judging any of them.

  • 1. Mood Wheel for Kids

    A colorful wheel children spin to pick a story based on how they feel.

  • 2. Parent Filter Panel

    Parents apply multiple filters (age, theme, time, values) before seeing results.

  • 3. Daily Story Recommendation

    One story suggested each day based on reading history.

  • 4. Voice Assistant Selection

    Parents speak preferences and the app suggests a story.

  • 5. Story Cards Shuffle

    Swipe through flashcard-style story previews until one feels right.

  • 6. Values-Based Library

    Stories grouped into strong value categories like women empowerment, kindness, history, diversity.

  • 7. Time-Based Story Buckets

    Stories grouped only by how long they take to read (5, 10, 15 minutes).

  • 8. 3-Question Guided Flow

    Answer three simple questions → receive three perfectly matched story options.

Eight concepts, from a mood wheel to a values-based library.

Idea eight won, because it was the only one that served both people at once. A guided flow lets a parent set the constraints — time available, age — while the child still gets to make the choice that matters to them.

The concept

The three-tap model

Three questions, three answers, three story options. How much time do you have tonight? How old is the little dreamer? What vibe are you after?

It reduces the dilemma into a 3-tap decision flow that recommends three meaningful story options tailored to:

  • Available time
  • Child’s age
  • Desired theme or vibe (e.g. women empowerment, history, adventure, kindness)

This converts browsing into a guided decision.

The library still exists, but it is no longer the first thing a tired parent has to face.

Design process

From a sketch to a clickable prototype

The same screen carried through three levels of fidelity. A clickable prototype was built to simulate the full three-tap journey from opening the app to selecting a book — the target was under fifteen seconds.

Design system

Dark, warm, and legible at arm’s length

The app is used in a dim room by someone who is already half asleep, so the interface sits on near-black with warm coral and lilac accents rather than bright white. Inter carries the type, and the vibe tags are the one place colour is allowed to be playful, because that is the tap belonging to the child.

Usability testing

Five parents, three tasks

Testing was run with five parents of children aged 3 to 11 to evaluate how well the story-selection experience actually worked.

Tasks given

  • Select a story for a 7-year-old with 10 minutes available
  • Choose a story focused on nature
  • Complete the flow without guidance

What happened

  • All users completed the task in under 20 seconds
  • Parents loved that kids could answer the vibe question themselves
  • No user tried to browse — they followed the flow naturally
  • “I wish this existed in real life.”

What changed

Three fixes that came out of testing

Moods became themes

“Happy, energetic, low on energy” was hard for children to answer.

Asking a child how they feel turns out to be a harder question than it sounds. I reframed the options into theme-based categories — Magical, Nature, Space, History — which are easier to recognise and far more intuitive to pick from.

A way out of the guided flow

Some families wanted to explore rather than always be guided.

I added an optional Browse Library feature so users can view and select from the full collection when they want more control. The guided flow stays the default, but it is no longer the only door.

“Search” became “Search your vibe”

A generic search label did not match how children think about choosing stories.

Changing four words made the search bar read as part of the same conversation as the rest of the app, rather than a database lookup bolted onto it.

Outcome

What it added up to

  • A three-tap decision model replacing open-ended browsing
  • A full iPad flow from opening screen to story completion
  • A clickable prototype covering the journey end to end
  • A dark-mode design system built for a dim room
  • Usability testing with five parents, and three changes that came from it

The sprint reached a selected story in under twenty seconds, against a problem that had been costing families more time than the reading itself.

Reflection

What I took away

The brief I was given was about stories. The problem was about choosing. Once the competitor analysis made clear that nobody was short of content, the whole project got simpler — and a lot more useful.

The mood question is the part I think about most. It was the idea I was most attached to, it tested badly, and the fix was not a better interface but a better question. Children could not tell me how they felt, but they could tell me they wanted something magical.
