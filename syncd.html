Spend less time tracking

What is Syncd?

Syncd is a subscription management dashboard. It brings every recurring payment into one place so people can see what they are paying for, what is about to renew, and what they no longer use — without digging through bank statements or app stores.

Role
UX/UI Designer
Timeline
2026
Scope
Research through to tested prototype
Tools

The brief

A dashboard for a new market

As Syncd planned its expansion into Germany, the company wanted a subscription management dashboard built for adults aged 30 and over. The challenge was to help those users track and stay in control of their subscription spending through a simple, intuitive experience.

That meant two things had to be true at once: the product had to feel trustworthy enough for people to connect their finances to it, and simple enough that checking a renewal took seconds rather than minutes.

Research

People only notice a subscription when it charges them

I ran a survey with 20 participants — adults aged 30 and over, a mix of working professionals and families. I wanted to understand how they currently manage subscriptions, where that breaks down, and what they fall back on when it does.

  • People forget about subscriptions until they are charged for them.
  • Unexpected charges are the single biggest source of frustration.
  • People want to understand where their money is actually going.
  • They want every subscription in one place rather than scattered across apps.
  • Trust is non-negotiable for anything touching their finances.

91% felt overwhelmed managing multiple subscriptions, 82% had no clear view of what they were paying for, and 80% found cancelling harder than it should be. The problem was not that people were careless with money. It was that nothing gave them a single, honest view of it.

From need to opportunity

Four needs, four things to build

The survey findings resolved into four plain user needs, and each one pointed at a feature. Keeping them paired like this kept the scope honest — nothing went into the product that could not be traced back to something someone had actually said.

The system

A palette that reads as a financial tool

Because trust was the thing users said they needed most, the interface had to look considered rather than playful. The palette runs from a deep navy through to a bright cyan, used sparingly for emphasis, on an almost-white background. Plus Jakarta Sans carries the whole product — a single family across headings, figures and labels, so numbers stay legible at small sizes.

Red routes

Three journeys that had to work

Rather than designing every screen, I identified the three journeys people would actually repeat — and designed those end to end.

Check all subscriptions, and cancel one

The two things people said they could never do quickly.

Everything starts from the home page. From there a user can either follow an alert into a subscription they did not expect, or browse the full list. Both paths land on the same subscription detail screen, so there is one place where a subscription lives and one place to act on it. Where a service has to be cancelled through its own website, the app says so plainly and walks through the steps rather than pretending it can do it.

Check the month’s total spending

“Users want to understand where their money is going.”

Spending is broken down by category, then by individual service, with a calendar view showing when each charge lands. The aim was to answer the question people were really asking — not “how much do I spend” but “what is coming out, and when.”

Designing for the market

Language and currency preferences

To support the expansion into Germany, I designed a localisation feature that lets users change their language and preferred currency. Financial information shown in an unfamiliar format is not just inconvenient — it quietly undermines the trust the rest of the product is trying to earn. Seeing Abo hinzufügen and Euro € rather than a translated-looking English screen is what makes the product feel local rather than exported.

Usability testing

Round one: cancelling was still too hard

I tested with five users, asking each to locate upcoming payments, cancel a subscription, and check their monthly spending.

The dashboard itself tested well. Participants found it intuitive and easy to navigate, and singled out the clean layout, the budget overview and the visibility of upcoming payments. The problems were all in the flow around cancelling.

  • The cancellation flow was too complex. People expected cancelling to be quick and found it long and cumbersome. The extra steps created friction and reduced their confidence that it had actually worked.
  • Cancellation actions lacked visibility. Multiple cancel buttons across different screens left users unsure which was the real one.
  • Navigation did not match expectations. Several people expected to manage subscriptions straight from the dashboard rather than moving through screens to get there — a mismatch between the product’s structure and their mental model.

Usability testing

Round two: what changed

I simplified the cancellation flow, cut the duplicate actions down to one clear primary button per screen, and retested with three participants.

  • Subscription management became intuitive. All participants managed and cancelled subscriptions without confusion, locating the action quickly and completing it with confidence.
  • Dashboard navigation felt clear. Users found subscriptions and upcoming payments without difficulty.
  • Spending insights still needed better visibility. Some participants struggled to find detailed spending information and expected a clearer way into the breakdown — the next thing I would work on.

The result

The finished dashboard

Outcome

What it added up to

  • A centralised dashboard covering every recurring payment
  • Smart alerts for renewals and price increases
  • Spending analytics broken down by category and by month
  • A guided cancellation flow, rebuilt after testing
  • Language and currency localisation for the German market
  • Two rounds of usability testing with eight participants

The thing testing made obvious is that a financial product is judged on its hardest moment, not its prettiest screen. People liked the dashboard immediately — but they only trusted it once cancelling a subscription took three taps instead of seven.

Reflection

What I took away

Designing for a market I do not live in forced me to be careful about what I was assuming. Currency formatting, the wording of a cancellation notice, what counts as a reassuring amount of detail — none of those translate cleanly, and all of them affect whether someone trusts a product with their money.

It also taught me to test the uncomfortable part of a product first. The dashboard was never the risk. The risk was the moment a user tries to leave, and that is exactly the flow I had spent the least time on.
